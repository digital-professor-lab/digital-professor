Course Structure (6 of 14 modules shown)

ModuleGoal
M1 · Functions & Mathematical ModelingRefresh core function types and show how functions model biological/social phenomena.
M3 · The Derivative: Definition & Rates of ChangeIntroduce the derivative as instantaneous rate of change, motivated by real rate questions.
M4 · Differentiation RulesBuild the mechanical toolkit — power, product, quotient, chain rules. (Quiz 1)
M8 · OptimizationUse derivatives to find and justify optima in applied bio/social problems.
M12 · Definite Integral & the FTCFormalize accumulated change via Riemann sums and the Fundamental Theorem. (Quiz 4)
M14 · Differential Equations & Logistic GrowthExtend growth modeling to resource-limited (logistic) growth. (Portfolio 2 due)

Week 15 is reserved for cumulative review and the final exam. Quizzes, group projects, and portfolios are woven into the module timeline exactly where the intake said they occur.

A Fully Expanded Topic (Module 4 · The Chain Rule)

Core Concepts
  • Composite functions; the "outside/inside" structure
  • Statement of the chain rule
  • Chain rule with multiple nested layers
Learning Objectives
  1. Explain the chain rule as tracking how a composite function's rate of change compounds.
  2. Apply the chain rule to nested/composite functions, including modeling contexts.
Why the differentiation rules are historically hard. Notational overload across three new rules in one week, and the chain rule's "outside/inside" structure is easy to misidentify — leading to systematic errors.
Mitigation. Use a consistent color-coded/boxed "outside/inside" annotation on every example, and run explicit common-error walkthroughs comparing a wrong application side by side with the correct one.
Example Questions
Foundational Differentiate $f(x) = 3x^4 - 5x^2 + 7$.Answer: $f'(x) = 12x^3 - 10x$.
Applied A drug concentration is $C(t) = \dfrac{5t}{t^2+4}$ (mg/L). Find $C'(t)$ and interpret its sign at $t=1$ and $t=3$.Answer: quotient rule gives $C'(1)>0$ (still rising) and $C'(3)<0$ (falling) — tying the derivative's sign to the physical process.
