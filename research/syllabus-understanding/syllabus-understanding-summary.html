Can AI Understand a Full One-Semester Course?

An experiment testing how well large language models can read a course's syllabus and/or source materials and reconstruct a complete, semester-scoped breakdown — modules, concepts, learning objectives, difficulty, and prerequisites.

1The Goal

The core question driving this part of the project.

Objective: “Explore how AI can understand and break down a full one-semester course using a syllabus and/or professor-provided materials.”

For a digital/AI professor to teach a course, it first has to understand the shape of the course: what topics are covered, in what order, at what depth, with what prerequisites, and where students typically struggle. This experiment measures how reliably today's AI models can produce that structured understanding — and which inputs make them do it best.

2Results at a Glance

The headline outcomes of this work.

  • An interactive breakdown prompt. We turned course-breakdown creation into an interactive process: the model interviews the instructor about the course before designing it, rather than relying on a static document.
  • Authored by Claude, portable to GPT. The prompt was written by Claude, but it is model-agnostic — we produced working breakdowns with it in both Claude and GPT.
  • It cracks high-level courses. Unlike the initial, non-interactive prompt, the interactive version breaks down high-level courses (PhD, Master's) substantially better.
  • Validated across 7 courses — all successful. We tested it on 6 high-level courses — one of them designed live with the actual professor — plus 1 undergraduate course to confirm it still performs at that level. Every one succeeded.
Bottom line: an interviewer-style prompt, portable across Claude and GPT, produces reliable full-course breakdowns even for advanced graduate courses — see the prompt itself (§3) and the worked examples (§11).

3The Interactive Prompt

The prompt that drives the whole method — authored by Claude, runnable in Claude or GPT.

Tell-tale evidence in the file set: the _Book_ (full-textbook) files exist only for Calc1, and only for 3 of the 4 models. The _Content_ (table-of-contents) files exist for all three courses and all four models — the textbook method was tried once, judged unworkable, and dropped in favour of the table of contents, which in turn revealed its own scope problem (§7).

6Why the Full-Textbook Method Didn't Work

Feeding the whole book seemed like it should give the richest result. It didn't.

  • Courses use more than one textbook. The PhD Number Theory course draws on several references (the breakdowns cross-cite them as [W] [N] [L]). There is no single "the textbook" to feed — and stitching multiple full books together is impractical.
  • A full textbook is too large to parse reliably. Entire books strain the context window and the model's ability to hold the whole thing in view. This hits free-tier models hardest — Gemini's full-textbook run for Calc1 collapsed to just 121 lines, a fraction of what it produced from a table of contents.
  • The model summarizes the book, not the course. A textbook contains far more than fits in one semester. Fed the whole thing, models drift into re-summarizing chapters and then arbitrarily trim — Claude's full-book Calc1 output silently restricted itself to “Chapters 1–6,” with no principled reason tied to the actual semester.
  • Low signal-to-noise. Worked examples, exercises, and prose add tokens without adding structural information about what the course covers — which is the only thing needed at this stage.
Bottom line: the textbook method maximized input size but not useful input. It was slower, more expensive, degraded badly on free tiers, and produced course maps that were less faithful to the actual semester than a plain syllabus-driven approach.

7The Table-of-Contents Trap: Overshooting the Course

A table of contents fixes the textbook method's size problem — but introduces a new one: it cannot tell the model what the course actually covers.

  • It did solve the parsing problem. A ToC is small and structured — chapters and sections only — so it feeds cleanly even on free tiers and handles multiple books at once (the [W] [N] [L] multi-source correspondence in the Number Theory breakdowns). On that axis it clearly beats the full textbook.
  • But a ToC lists everything the book contains — not what the professor teaches. There is no signal about which chapters are on the syllabus and which are skipped, so the model cannot separate covered material from ignored material.
  • Concrete failure — the PhD course. Number Theory I drew on its reference books for only a handful of topics, yet the ToC-based breakdowns assumed the entire books were taught — inflating the course far beyond its real one-semester scope.
  • "In the book" gets treated as "in the course." That assumption is wrong for almost any selective course — and advanced courses are nearly always selective.
Bottom line: a table of contents tells you what could be covered, not what is covered. Without the syllabus to bound it, the model over-scopes the course.

11Further Development — Interactive Intake for High-Level Courses

After presenting the results above, the next question was: how do we break down high-level courses (Master's, PhD) — which frequently have no fixed syllabus, or draw selectively on several books?

The syllabus method depends on a syllabus existing. For advanced or newly-designed courses that often isn't true. So I built an interactive prompt that interviews the instructor about the course they plan to teach — level, format, duration, assessment structure, assumed background, project count — and then generates the full course-design package from those answers.

It keeps the scope-fidelity win. The reason the syllabus beat the table of contents (§7) is that it states the course's real scope. An instructor interview captures exactly that — straight from the person who defines the scope — even when no syllabus document exists. And when the instructor leaves something unspecified, the model states the assumption explicitly instead of inventing coverage.

12Verdict & Next Steps

Best input method: the syllabus — or, at minimum, an explicit list of the topics to teach or learn. It is the only input that defines the course's actual scope. The full textbook failed (§6), and a table of contents overshoots because it cannot tell covered material from skipped material (§7). A syllabus encodes exactly what the professor intends to teach, so the breakdown stays inside the real course.
Where a table of contents still helps: once the syllabus has fixed the scope, a ToC (or the book itself) can enrich the depth within those chosen topics. Scope should come from the syllabus; detail can come from the book — but never scope from the book alone.
Scaling to courses without a syllabus: for high-level (Master's/PhD) or newly-designed courses, an interactive instructor interview (§11) captures the same real-scope signal a syllabus provides — extending the recommended approach to cases where no syllabus document exists yet.
Best model: Claude (Pro) is the quality benchmark — most faithful, best on the hardest (PhD) course. Among free models, GPT leads on completeness and Perplexity on balance; Gemini lags.

Where this points next

  • Make the syllabus / explicit topic list the primary input for every course, passed as a hard scope constraint on the breakdown.
  • When richer detail is wanted, add the table of contents on top of the syllabus (syllabus for scope, ToC for depth) — never the ToC alone.
  • Define an objective rubric — scope fidelity first (does the breakdown stay within the syllabus?), plus prerequisite accuracy and difficulty calibration — to score breakdowns rather than judging by eye.
  • Test whether combining models — e.g. GPT for exhaustive coverage, Claude for scoping and structure — beats any single model.
