\section{Course Description and Design Rationale}
%==================================================================

\emph{Calculus for Biology and Medicine} (4th ed.) is written for a one- or two-semester
calculus sequence for life-science and social-science majors. Its twelve chapters run from
precalculus review through probability and statistics, and the authors explicitly design
sections marked with an asterisk (\opt) as optional ``instructor's choice'' material that
can be trimmed without breaking the logical flow of the book.

This document treats \textbf{Chapters 1--6} as \emph{Calculus 1}: precalculus review,
discrete-time (recurrence) models, limits and continuity, differentiation, applications of
differentiation, and integration through the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. This mirrors
how the book is used in two-semester bio-calculus tracks (e.g., the first course covers
single-variable differential and integral calculus, and a second course, \emph{Calculus 2},
covers Chapters 7--12: integration techniques, differential equations, linear algebra,
multivariable calculus, systems of differential equations, and probability/statistics --
see Appendix~\ref{app:calc2}). Instructors on a one-semester-only track can compress the
schedule in Section~\ref{sec:schedule} by cutting all asterisked (\opt) subsections.

\textbf{Distinguishing feature of this course:} unlike a generic ``applied calculus''
course, every mathematical tool is introduced through a biological or social-science model
first (population growth, drug absorption and elimination, enzyme kinetics, epidemics,
allometric scaling, blood alcohol elimination, oxygen binding in blood) and only then
treated with full mathematical rigor. Word-problem literacy -- turning a verbal description
of a process into an equation -- is therefore treated as a first-class course objective,
not an afterthought.

\section{Course-Level Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)}
%==================================================================

By the end of the course, a successful student will be able to:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{SLO\arabic*.}, leftmargin=2.6em]
\item \textbf{Model discrete and continuous change.} Translate a verbal description of a
      biological or social process into a recurrence equation, a limit statement, a
      derivative, or a definite integral, and interpret the mathematical result back in
      the original context.
\item \textbf{Evaluate limits and determine continuity} of algebraic, exponential,
      logarithmic, and trigonometric functions, including limits at infinity and limits
      that require the sandwich theorem.
\item \textbf{Differentiate} elementary, polynomial, rational, power, exponential,
      logarithmic, and trigonometric functions using the formal definition and the rules
      of differentiation (sum, product, quotient, chain, implicit).
\item \textbf{Apply derivatives} to find extrema, analyze monotonicity and concavity,
      solve optimization and related-rates problems, evaluate indeterminate forms
      (L'H\^{o}pital's Rule), and produce derivative-informed graphs.
\item \textbf{Evaluate definite and indefinite integrals}, state and apply both parts of
      the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and interpret a definite integral as
      cumulative change or an average value.
\item \textbf{Use technology} (graphing calculators or spreadsheets) to visualize
      functions and data, iterate recurrence equations, and implement simple numerical
      methods (bisection, Newton--Raphson).
\item \textbf{Communicate quantitative reasoning}, including the assumptions behind a
      mathematical model, its limitations, and how well it fits real or simulated data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Prerequisite Topics (Course Entry Requirements)}
\label{sec:prereq}
%==================================================================

Formal prerequisite: a passing grade in Algebra II / Precalculus, or a satisfactory score
on a placement/diagnostic exam. Chapter 1 of the text (\S1.1--\S1.4) is built into Week 1--2
of the schedule as an explicit, graded review rather than an assumption, since incoming
preparation varies widely between life-science and social-science students. Students should
arrive able to:

\begin{itemize}
\item Perform algebraic manipulation of real numbers, absolute values, and inequalities.
\item Write the equation of a line (point--slope, slope--intercept, standard form) and a circle.
\item Convert between degrees and radians and recall basic trigonometric identities.
\item Apply the laws of exponents and logarithms; solve simple exponential/log equations.
\item Solve linear and quadratic equations, including via the quadratic formula.
\item Identify domain, range, and composition of a function from a formula or graph.
\item Recognize and sketch polynomial, rational, power, exponential, logarithmic,
      and trigonometric functions, and apply basic graph transformations.
\end{itemize}

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, colback=soft, colframe=secondary, arc=2pt, boxrule=0.5pt,
  title={\bfseries Built-in Diagnostic}, fonttitle=\bfseries, coltitle=white, colbacktitle=secondary]
Section 1.1, \emph{Precalculus Skills Diagnostic Test}, should be assigned before the first
class meeting (self-graded or auto-graded in MyLab Math). Students who miss more than
20\% of items are directed to the matching subsection of \S1.2--\S1.4 for targeted review
during Week 1--2 office hours / recitation.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{diffbox}
\textbf{The chain rule (\S4.5)} and \textbf{related rates (\S4.6.2)} are the two most
commonly mis-executed skills in this module. The chain rule fails most often when
functions are triple-composed (e.g., $e^{\sin(2x)}$); related rates fail most often at the
translation step (turning a verbal scenario into a differentiable equation) rather than at
the differentiation step itself. Dedicate at least one full recitation to each, using a
``diagram-first'' strategy for related rates (label all time-dependent quantities before
differentiating).
\end{diffbox}

\begin{diffbox}
\textbf{Optimization word problems (\S5.4)} are consistently the highest-friction topic in

identify the quantity to optimize, (b) express it in one variable using a constraint, and
(c) justify that a critical point is a maximum/minimum in context. Model 2--3 full
worked examples end-to-end (including a sanity check of the answer) before assigning
homework.
\end{diffbox}

\subsection*{Modeling Project}
Working individually or in pairs, students choose a published or provided real dataset
(e.g., red-wolf population counts, fish-yield vs.\ chlorophyll data, or a drug-elimination
time series) and: (1) propose a model from Chapters 2, 4, or 5 (recurrence equation,
exponential/power fit via linearization, or a differential-equation description); (2)
estimate parameters from the data (using the log--log/semilog linearization technique of
\S1.4.3); (3) assess model fit and discuss its biological or social-science limitations.
This directly exercises SLO1, SLO6, and SLO7.

\section{Technology in the Course}
%==================================================================

Consistent with the textbook's own emphasis, this course assumes access to a graphing
calculator and asks students to use spreadsheet software (the authors recommend Google
Sheets for its shareability) for:
\begin{itemize}
\item Iterating recurrence equations numerically (\S2.2.2\opt, \S2.3).
\item Implementing the bisection method to solve for roots (\S3.5.2\opt).
\item Numerically approximating Riemann sums for the definite integral (\S6.1).

\end{itemize}
No programming background is assumed or required.
